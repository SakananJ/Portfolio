Analog & RF Design

Analog Radio Receiver Filter & Quadrature Mixer

The analog front end of a software-defined radio. It selects the 8 to 16 MHz band out of everything the antenna picks up, clamps the signal to a safe level, then downconverts it to baseband I/Q for the demodulator. As the first analog stage, any error it introduces propagates through the whole receiver and cannot be corrected later, so the design was driven by accuracy and ease of debugging.

The Signal Chain

An RF signal passes through a 4th-order Butterworth LC bandpass filter (8 to 16 MHz) and a ±0.7 V limiter. Baluns convert the single-ended output into the differential pair the mixer needs, and two AD8343 active mixers. One mixer fed a 0° local oscillator, one fed 90° - downconvert it to I and Q.

Butterworth beats Chebyshev here on flat, ripple-free passband, and being passive it adds no noise. On paper, the chain delivers 37 dB: 7 dB from the mixer plus 29.8 dB from the difference amplifiers.

Changing the Mixer Mid-Project

The original mixer was dropped as too difficult to hand-solder, and the AD8343 replaced it. However, the AD8343 needs differential RF and LO inputs, so baluns were implemented. Its gain is 7 dB instead of 17 dB, so the amplifier had to make up the difference. A 200 to 500 Ω load against the 16.4 kΩ was needed, so a parallel resistor went across the output.

Stage 1

Simulation in LTSpice

I made the Butterworth filter and simulated a frequency sweep in LTSpice. With 50 Ω source/load, the passband is at -6 dB, with the 8 MHz and 16 MHz points being exactly -3 dB below passband, meaning it met the requirements in simulation. A transient run confirmed the diodes clamping at ±0.7 V.

Stage 2

Breadboard Prototype

The lab did not stock every calculated value, so each component was built up from what was available. 750 nH from 560 + 180 nH, 270 pF from four caps in parallel.

I swept the filter from 1 to 20 MHz and confirmed that signals within the 8 - 16 MHz passband were passed, while frequencies outside the passband were strongly attenuated. The amplifier increased the signal from 200 mV peak-to-peak to 2.25 V peak-to-peak, corresponding to a gain of around 21 dB.

Stage 3

Schematic Capture and PCB Layout

The design moved into Altium as a schematic and board layout, which had to defend two things: I/Q symmetry, since trace length mismatch destroys the 90° relationship, and isolation, since 16 MHz signals couple readily through parasitic capacitance.

Stage 4

Bring-Up, and Everything That Went Wrong

Three problems surfaced before a single measurement. J2 and J3 were swapped in the layout - caught only after fabrication, so the board had to be flywired onto the mainboard. The header pins went on crooked, which was solved by resoldering. The I-channel mixer was damaged and could not be fixed in time, which was considered during testing.

Frequency response was then captured by sweeping the input and logging gain with a Python script.

Stage 5

Filter Results: Right Band, Wrong Shape

The Python sweep put the passband at 8 to 11 MHz with fluctuating gain. The oscilloscope's frequency response analysis gave the correct 8 to 16 MHz limits and smooth attenuation outside the band, but gave a passband that was not flat, with an incorrect -3 dB cutoff.

The cause was physical, not electrical. The filter inductors sat too close and magnetically coupled, while long ground and flywire runs added parasitic inductance the schematic knew nothing about. To fix this later, it needs to be spaced out and shorten the ground return.

Stage 6

Gain Results: The Breadboard Lied

Gain peaked at ~27 dB against the 37 dB target, and the chain behaved like a bandpass instead of holding constant gain. Attenuation past 96 kHz was smooth, and I and Q tracked each other closely.

The root cause is that the 1 mH inductors used as RF chokes were acting as high-pass filters, attenuating signals that were needed.

Stage 7

I/Q Balance and Phase

Amplitude balance stayed inside ±1 dB across the sweep, since both channels are the identical circuit, differing only by the 90° shift on the local oscillator.

Phase returned no valid data. With the post-mixer filter attenuating the signal, the outputs were too weak to extract a phase relationship from, so the 90° requirement could not be confirmed either way.

Verdict

Three of six requirements passed: the band limits, the ±0.7 V limiter, and I/Q amplitude mismatch. Passband flatness, overall gain, and phase shift failed.

With critical requirements unmet, this radio filter and mixer would have produced misleading results for the radio system. The next spin is scoped: space the BPF inductors, replace the chokes with an RC network, correct the pin orientation, recalculate the op-amp gain, and retest.
